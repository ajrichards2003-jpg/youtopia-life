Strength & cognition

Beyond the gym. Into a more difficult question.

A well-studied training supplement has become a memory conversation. The evidence for these two uses is at different stages.

The muscle evidence and the memory question deserve separate headlines.The Youtopia perspective · editorial interpretation

Creatine is unusual in the supplement world: its familiar training role gives a newer brain hypothesis an immediate audience. That familiarity can help research receive attention. It can also make early cognitive findings feel more established than they are.

The Youtopia read

Creatine monohydrate can support strength and repeated high-intensity efforts, particularly alongside resistance training. Small cognitive studies report possible memory or processing benefits, sometimes more apparent in older adults, but results conflict. It is not an established treatment for memory complaints or dementia prevention.

What it does for the body

Creatine helps replenish energy during brief, intense muscle work. Trials support gains in strength, power and training capacity; it adds little to endurance performance. The practical partner is progressive resistance training, with enough food, protein and recovery—not a complicated supplement stack.

What we know about the mind

The brain also uses creatine in energy metabolism. A 2023 meta-analysis reported improved memory measures, especially among older adults aged 66–76. Later reviews found mixed findings across cognitive tasks and limited certainty. An improvement on a test does not prove that a person’s everyday forgetfulness will improve, and no trial establishes creatine as a way to prevent dementia.

The dementia study that changed the conversation

A 2025 pilot enrolled 20 people with Alzheimer’s disease for eight weeks. Brain creatine increased and several cognitive tests improved, which makes brain energy worth studying. The study had no placebo group. Repeating a cognitive test, receiving extra attention, or other changes over time could contribute to the result. A larger controlled trial is the next step before calling creatine a dementia treatment.

That distinction matters for families. Creatine’s established role in resistance training can be discussed separately from an emerging brain hypothesis. Persistent or worsening forgetfulness deserves a clinical assessment rather than a supplement experiment alone.

A practical, cautious approach

Creatine monohydrate is the most studied form. In exercise research, a common maintenance intake is 3–5 g daily; loading is optional for most people and may increase stomach upset. A convenient consistent time with food or water is reasonable. There is no proven “brain dose” or special timing for memory. Expect possible water-related weight gain. If kidney disease, abnormal kidney tests or medicines affecting the kidneys are involved, discuss it with a clinician before use; tell them about supplementation when interpreting creatinine tests.

For someone who feels slower to recall things, start with the symptom rather than the tub: sleep, mood, medicines, hearing, B12 status and thyroid problems can all matter. A GP can help identify treatable causes. See our memory guide for a way to make that conversation useful.

What a pilot can genuinely contribute

A small study can answer practical questions about feasibility, measurements and whether a signal is worth pursuing. It is a necessary part of research, not a lesser version of a completed treatment trial. The Alzheimer’s pilot belongs in that category.

For a family reading the headline, the missing comparison is crucial. Without a placebo group, improvement during a study cannot be confidently attributed to the supplement. The next experiment needs to test that attribution, not simply repeat the positive description.

Two goals, two conversations

Our interpretation is to keep a resistance-training decision separate from a memory-care decision. The first can draw on a substantial exercise literature. The second needs an assessment of the symptoms and a careful reading of emerging trials. Neither benefits from an invented universal brain dose. We will watch for controlled replication, everyday functional outcomes and a clear account of who might benefit, rather than treating a familiar ingredient as a shortcut around those questions.
