Understand what you take.

A practical map of the body's process, likely benefit, timing and amounts. Start with food, a clear purpose and a medication check. This is a growing library; the existing quick reference covers more ingredients.

Amounts below describe adult dietary reference intakes or studied protocols, not a personal prescription. Needs change with pregnancy, illness, medicines, age and test results. A high dose in a trial does not mean a better dose for everyone.
Reviewed entry

Vitamin D3

How it works in the body
Fat-soluble D3 is absorbed with dietary fat, converted in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, then activated mainly in kidneys. It helps regulate calcium and phosphate absorption and bone mineralisation.
What it may improve
Correcting inadequate status and supporting bone health; benefits outside deficiency depend on the outcome and population.
When and with what
Food containing fat; morning or evening is a preference. Consider calcium intake from food.
Amount and context
Adult recommended intake is commonly 600 IU (15 µg) through age 70 and 800 IU (20 µg) after 70. A blood test can guide treatment when deficiency is suspected.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
Trials of 4,000 IU/day for diabetes prevention did not establish broad prevention. The adult upper limit is 4,000 IU/day unless supervised; more can cause high calcium.

Magnesium

How it works in the body
A cofactor in hundreds of enzyme reactions, including energy production, nerve signalling and muscle contraction. The gut absorbs it and the kidneys regulate excess.
What it may improve
Meeting needs supports normal function; sleep benefit from extra magnesium in people without deficiency is less certain.
When and with what
With food if it upsets the stomach. Glycinate and citrate are formulations; count elemental magnesium across both.
Amount and context
Adult dietary requirements vary by age and sex, roughly 310–420 mg/day from all sources. Supplement amount is a separate decision.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
The U.S. adult upper limit of 350 mg/day applies to supplemental magnesium, not food. Higher doses are used medically for specific conditions, with monitoring; they are not a proven general sleep upgrade.

Zinc

How it works in the body
Absorbed in the small intestine and used by enzymes, immune signalling, wound repair and gene expression. The body has no specialised long-term zinc store.
What it may improve
Corrects low intake; zinc lozenges have been studied for shortening colds, with product and timing differences.
When and with what
Food can reduce nausea; separate from certain antibiotics. Consider copper status if a clinician prescribes extended high doses.
Amount and context
Adult recommended intake is about 8 mg/day for women and 11 mg/day for men.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
High-dose zinc is not a routine immune strategy. The adult upper limit is 40 mg/day from all sources; sustained excess can produce copper deficiency.

Vitamin C

How it works in the body
A water-soluble antioxidant and enzyme cofactor required to make collagen and carnitine. It improves absorption of non-heme iron from plant foods.
What it may improve
Prevents deficiency and helps normal tissue repair; regular supplementation may modestly shorten colds in some studies but does not reliably prevent them in the general population.
When and with what
Any time; combine plant iron foods with citrus or capsicum. Food-first intake is practical.
Amount and context
Adult recommended intake is 75 mg/day for women and 90 mg/day for men; smokers need more.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
Gram-dose trials have not established a general longevity benefit. The adult upper limit is 2,000 mg/day; gastrointestinal upset and kidney-stone considerations matter.

Omega-3 EPA/DHA

How it works in the body
EPA and DHA incorporate into cell membranes and contribute to signalling molecules that regulate inflammation and lipid metabolism.
What it may improve
Fish intake and prescription formulations have different evidence. Specific high-dose prescription EPA can help selected cardiovascular-risk groups; triglycerides can fall at therapeutic doses.
When and with what
With a meal. Read the actual EPA and DHA content, not only total fish-oil milligrams.
Amount and context
There is no single universal EPA/DHA supplement dose for healthy adults; dietary fish guidance and individual goals differ.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
Prescription 4 g/day regimens are studied for high triglycerides and selected risks, but are not equivalent to over-the-counter capsules. Higher-dose trials also signal atrial-fibrillation risk in some groups.

Creatine monohydrate

How it works in the body
Muscle and brain cells convert creatine to phosphocreatine, a rapid reserve for regenerating ATP during short, intense effort.
What it may improve
Strongest evidence is for repeated high-intensity effort and resistance-training gains. Cognition findings are interesting but mixed.
When and with what
Daily consistency matters more than a clock time; take with food if preferred. Pair with progressive training and adequate protein.
Amount and context
Studies commonly use 3–5 g/day maintenance in adults. A loading phase is optional, not required for eventual saturation.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
Some studies use 20 g/day divided for 5–7 days to load stores faster; this is not evidence that chronic 20 g/day gives extra benefit. Discuss kidney disease or abnormal lab values with a clinician.

Vitamin B12

How it works in the body
Absorbed after stomach acid releases it from food and intrinsic factor enables uptake in the ileum. It supports red blood cells, myelin and DNA synthesis.
What it may improve
Correcting deficiency can reverse anaemia and prevent neurologic harm. Vegans and some older adults are at higher risk.
When and with what
Any time; oral, sublingual and injections serve different clinical situations. Metformin and acid-suppressing medicines can affect status.
Amount and context
Adult recommended intake is 2.4 µg/day; deficiency treatment often uses much larger oral doses or injections under clinical direction.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
Large doses are sometimes used because only a fraction is absorbed. There is no established upper limit, but extra B12 does not reliably boost energy when status is adequate.

Iron

How it works in the body
Heme and non-heme iron enter through the gut, travel on transferrin and support haemoglobin, oxygen transport and energy enzymes. Hepcidin regulates absorption.
What it may improve
Iron replacement helps iron-deficiency anaemia and may improve fatigue when deficiency is established. Too much iron can be harmful.
When and with what
Vitamin C-rich food can improve non-heme absorption. Tea, coffee, calcium and some medicines can interfere; dosing schedules should follow a clinician's plan.
Amount and context
As dietary reference points, adults 19–50 need about 8 mg/day (men) or 18 mg/day (women); pregnancy raises the need. Blood counts and ferritin should guide iron supplements rather than a universal pill dose.
Higher-dose evidence and limits
Therapeutic elemental iron doses are higher than dietary requirements and can cause gastrointestinal effects. There is no reason to take high-dose iron without a clear indication.
